## Counsel
Take the medicine your doctor prescribed, and keep taking it under his care — nothing in our confessions teaches that antidepressants confess a failed faith, and quiet shame is a poor reason to overrule a physician. Tell someone this week — your spouse or a trusted brother or sister, and your pastor: the hiding, more than the prescription, is what is out of step with our way, because the body cannot carry a burden it cannot see. Let prayer and medicine be allies — keep both, and drop the ledger that scores one against the other. Do not adjust or stop the prescription on your own; and if the darkness ever moves toward harming yourself, treat that as an emergency: tell your doctor and someone close the same day.

## Grounding
- The three confessions do not address mental illness or its medicines (certain, as an honest statement of their silence).
- Galatians 6:2 (certain): bear one another's burdens.
- 1 Kings 19:4–8 (certain): the despairing prophet tended first with food and sleep.
- Confession of Faith in a Mennonite Perspective, articles on creation and providence and on stewardship/mutual aid (probable: Arts. 5, 21): care and healing as gifts held in common.

## Idiom
Among us illness was never unbelief — we call the doctor and we tell the church; carry this in the light, with the body, and receive the medicine like bread.

## Notes
Silence, marked: the counsel extends from mutual burden-bearing, the goodness of created remedies, and our practice of communal care; no article addresses the case itself. Across the spectrum Anabaptist communities use physicians; where stigma about mental health exists it is culture, not counsel.
