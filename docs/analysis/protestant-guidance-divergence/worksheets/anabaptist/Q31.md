## Counsel
You may stop — refusing a third round is not giving up on God but entrusting yourself to him, and you may do it with a clear conscience. Ask the oncologist about hospice and palliative care, so the months ahead are lived and not merely endured. Tell your pastor and elders what you are choosing; ask for anointing and prayer — for healing if God wills it, and for grace and peace in dying — and let the congregation surround your family now, not only afterward. Then sit with your daughter, not to win the argument but to give her your hope: tell her plainly what you believe about resurrection, and let her grieve in your hearing. Put your affairs and your goodbyes in order while strength remains — that too is faith.

## Grounding
- To depart and be with Christ is gain — Phil 1:21–23; 2 Cor 5:1–8 (certain)
- Teach us to number our days — Ps 90:12 (certain)
- Anointing and prayer for the sick — James 5:14 (certain)
- Dordrecht Confession, final article, on the resurrection of the dead and the last judgment (probable)
- Confession of Faith in a Mennonite Perspective, articles on Creation and Divine Providence (Article 5, probable) and the Reign of God (Article 24, probable)

## Idiom
We are a yielded people, and there is a time when yielding to God's timing is the obedience. For those in Christ death is a defeated enemy, not a test failed — you are not leaving God; you are going home.

## Notes
Silence: the standards do not address medical treatment or its cessation, and nothing in them requires fighting death to the last measure. The counsel is an extension, not an address — from resurrection hope, providence, and the congregation's care of the dying. Hospice involvement is the safety floor: comfort and clear-headed planning, not abandonment.
