## Counsel
Testify, and tell the whole truth — that duty is not in question. But when the clerk asks you to swear, ask to affirm instead: the law provides for affirmation, and courts accommodate it every day; you can tell the clerk beforehand, simply, "I don't swear oaths for religious reasons — I will affirm." If anyone asks why, one sentence is enough: your word is the same everywhere, so you may not add an oath to it. Then be the most careful, exact witness in the room — under affirmation, that is the whole point.

## Grounding
- Do not swear at all; let your yes be yes — Matt 5:33–37 (certain)
- Above all, do not swear... let your yes be yes, lest you fall under condemnation — James 5:12 (certain)
- Schleitheim Confession, Article 7, on the oath (certain) — swearing forbidden to those who belong to Christ, truth-telling commanded
- Dordrecht Confession, article on the swearing of oaths (Article 15, probable) — testimony given in truthful yes and no, oaths refused
- CFMP, article on Truth and the Avoidance of Oaths (Article 20, probable) — affirmation in place of swearing

## Idiom
An oath implies there are two grades of our speech — sworn, and less true. We keep only one grade. Affirm, and then speak as one whose every word was already under God.

## Notes
This is among the oldest and clearest counsels the standards give, uniform across the spectrum. Affirmation in place of the oath is the historic and legally recognized practice; no scruple requires you to obstruct the court or shade the truth.
