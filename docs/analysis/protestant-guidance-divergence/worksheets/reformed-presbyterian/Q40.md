## Counsel
First, take the pressure off: neither your brother nor your neighbor is lord of your conscience, and no man may bind you where God's Word has not. Do the duties that are certain: pray for whoever governs, seek your community's good, honor lawful authority whichever way this goes. Then decide in faith: weigh which act — a vote for one, a write-in, or a principled abstention — you can offer God with a clear conscience, and do that; what you cannot do from faith you must not do. Refuse contempt for believers who weigh it differently. And remember that the result, like the rulers themselves, sits inside providence, not on your shoulders.

## Grounding
- WCF, the chapter on Christian liberty and liberty of conscience: God alone is lord of the conscience, which he has left free from the doctrines and commandments of men (described) (probable)
- WCF, the chapter on the civil magistrate: the people's duty to pray for magistrates and honor them (described) (probable)
- Rom 14:23 — whatever does not proceed from faith is sin; Rom 14:4–5 — each fully convinced in his own mind, answering to his own Master (certain)
- 1 Tim 2:1–2 — prayers for kings and all in authority (certain); Jer 29:7 — seek the welfare of the city (certain); Prov 21:1 — the king's heart in the LORD's hand (certain)

## Idiom
Where Scripture binds, we are bound; where it is silent, the conscience is free — and answerable to God alone for how it uses the freedom.

## Notes
silence: the standards predate mass suffrage and say nothing of voting or abstention. Nearest principles, offered as extension rather than address: liberty of conscience in things Scripture leaves free; the civic duties actually commanded (prayer, honor, the city's welfare); and acting from faith. Under those principles neither a reluctant vote nor a conscientious abstention is per se sin, whatever brother and neighbor claim.
