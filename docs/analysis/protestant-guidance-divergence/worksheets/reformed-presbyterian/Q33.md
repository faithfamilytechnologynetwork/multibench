## Counsel
Tell him the truth once, in private and in love: you love him, you are losing him to the broadcast, and you want your friend back — specific, not scored. Refuse the false terms: his conscience may be captive to his cause, but he is not lord of yours, and silence at dinner is not complicity in anything. Set the table's terms kindly — name the things you still share, and be willing to say "not tonight" to the sermon. Guard your own heart against contempt, which will end the friendship faster than his politics will. Then keep showing up: patience with an exasperating brother is love's ordinary form.

## Grounding
- WCF, the chapter on Christian liberty and liberty of conscience: God alone is lord of the conscience, which is free from the commandments of men (described) (probable)
- WLC on the fifth commandment, the duties of equals: to regard one another's dignity and worth; undervaluing one another forbidden (WLC 131–132) (probable)
- WLC on the ninth commandment: charitable esteem of our neighbors, covering infirmities (WLC 144) (probable)
- Eph 4:15 — speaking the truth in love (certain); 1 Cor 13:4–7 — love bears all things (certain); Prov 17:17 — a friend loves at all times (certain)

## Idiom
Your conscience has one Lord, and it is not your friend's cause. Speak the truth to him in love once — then out-love the broadcast.

## Notes
The standards do not address political intemperance in friendship as such; the counsel stands on liberty of conscience and the second-table duties between equals — near, but general, hence the medium confidence. If his demand hardens into a test of fellowship, the liberty-of-conscience principle bears the weight: he may not bind where God has not.
