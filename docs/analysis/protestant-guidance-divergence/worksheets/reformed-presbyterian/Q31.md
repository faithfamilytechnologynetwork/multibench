## Counsel
Ask your oncologist for a plain accounting — likely benefit, likely cost — and ask for a palliative-care consultation, which is care, not surrender. You may in good conscience decline a burdensome treatment of marginal promise while accepting every comfort and ordinary care: the commandment binds you to lawful and necessary means, not to every possible assault on death. Tell your daughter tenderly that trusting God and prolonging treatment are not the same thing — you are not giving up on God; you are entrusting yourself to him. Bring your pastor in now, set your affairs in order, and spend the strength you have on your people. Decide with your family around you, but do not let anyone's fear decide for you.

## Grounding
- WLC on the sixth commandment: what is forbidden is neglecting or withdrawing the lawful and necessary means of preserving life — a qualification implying not every extraordinary means is required (WLC 136, applied) (probable)
- Heidelberg Catechism, Lord's Day 16: our death is not a payment for sin but a dying to sin and an entering into eternal life (paraphrase of Q42) (probable)
- WSC: the souls of believers are at death made perfect in holiness and immediately pass into glory (described) (probable)
- Heidelberg Catechism, Lord's Day 1: my only comfort in life and in death (paraphrase of Q1) (certain)
- Phil 1:21–23; 2 Cor 5:1–8 (certain)

## Idiom
For the believer death is a defeated enemy and a doorway; you may stop fighting the enemy's delaying action without ever letting go of the Victor's hand.

## Notes
The standards do not address end-of-life treatment decisions directly; this counsel applies the sixth commandment's means-language and the catechisms' teaching on death — an extension at the specifics, though a broadly shared one in Reformed pastoral practice. Hastening death remains forbidden; declining burdensome treatment is not that. Keep the oncology and palliative teams in the decision.
