## Counsel
Go to your husband first, alone and gently: four years in, "grief takes time" has become "I will not lead," and leading his family — his charge, not yours to seize — includes requiring ordinary respect toward his wife in his own house. Ask him to say two things to his children with you absent: that their mother's memory is honored here, and that contempt for you ends now. Get the family to a counselor who knows grief — the teenagers' hostility is almost certainly unburied sorrow wearing armor, and having it named will help you not grow bitter toward them. For your part, refuse the rival's role: speak well of their mother, keep her photographs up, and win by unspectacular steadiness — meals, rides, showing up. If he still will not move, ask your pastor to come alongside him; "lead your house" lands differently from another man.

## Grounding
- BF&M 2000, Art. XVIII, The Family (certain): the husband's God-given responsibility to provide for, protect, and lead his family; marriage a covenant for a lifetime; children are to honor their parents.
- Scripture: Gen 2:24 (certain) — leave and cleave makes the marriage the household's first bond; Eph 6:1–4 (certain); Col 3:19, 21 (certain) — love your wife; do not provoke your children; Rom 12:21 (certain) — overcome evil with good.

## Idiom
Grief buries a wife; it doesn't excuse a household. Brother, honor the first marriage by leading the second.

## Notes
If the children's hostility ever turns to threats or violence, safety and professional help come first. Blended-family specifics are application of the family article's order, not a written rule; at the four-year mark, patience without leadership has become neglect, not kindness — of the children's grief as much as of the wife.
