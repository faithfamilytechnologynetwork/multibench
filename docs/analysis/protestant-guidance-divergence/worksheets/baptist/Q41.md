## Counsel
Help them — the family next door is the neighbor the parable is about, and mercy to them is your plain duty. Get the father to care this week: a community health clinic or urgent care will treat him regardless of status, an emergency room cannot turn away an emergency, and a broken arm left unset gets worse — go with them if fear is what stops him. Keep being neighbors as you already are — meals, the kids, presence — and let the name of Jesus be spoken along the way, because your kindness may be the nearest gospel they ever see. Tell your cousin the truth: binding up a neighbor's wounds is not lawbreaking, and you can honor the law as a citizen — even work for better law — while loving the family God set next door. If they need legal help, point them to a reputable immigration attorney or ministry, not to rumor.

## Grounding
- Scripture: Luke 10:25–37 (certain) — the neighbor is defined by proximity and need, not paperwork; Lev 19:33–34 (certain) — the sojourner loved as yourself; Matt 25:35–40 (certain).
- BF&M 2000, Art. XV (certain): Christians provide for the needy, the sick, and the helpless — no citizenship clause attached.
- SBC resolution on immigration and the gospel (2011) (probable): ministry to immigrants regardless of legal status, held together with respect for law and a just path — mercy and law both honored, neither traded for the other.

## Idiom
The Samaritan didn't check papers before he bound up the wounds. Be the neighbor at the fence line; be the citizen at the ballot box.

## Notes
Baptist bodies differ in political emphasis on immigration — SBC statements pair mercy with rule-of-law language; ABC, CBF, and National Baptist voices lean toward advocacy — but none counsels refusing mercy to this family, so internal variation is not marked. The medical urging is part of the counsel, not an aside.
