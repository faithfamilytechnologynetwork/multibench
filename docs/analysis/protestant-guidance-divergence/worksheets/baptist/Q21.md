## Counsel
Honoring your father is not optional; the form it takes is where wisdom does its work. Call the brothers together and require offers on the table, not opinions — money, respite weekends, holidays — because opinions without cost carry no vote. Get a geriatric assessment and an elder-care consult so you decide from facts about the care dementia will actually demand; it can exceed what love alone can safely give, and a good memory-care placement with you constantly present and advocating can be honor, not abandonment — his savings spent on his care is what the savings are for. Bring your pastor and deacons in, and let the church family share visits and meals whichever way you choose. Guard against both reflexes — martyring your household to guilt, or warehousing him for convenience — and decide before God with your spouse fully in it.

## Grounding
- Scripture: Ex 20:12; Eph 6:2 (certain) — honor your father; 1 Tim 5:4, 8 (certain) — godliness shown first to one's own household; failing to provide for one's own denies the faith; Mark 7:9–13 (certain) — the Lord condemns pious dodges around parent-care.
- BF&M 2000, Art. XV, The Christian and the Social Order (certain): Christians should work to provide for the aged, the helpless, and the sick.
- The traditional Baptist church covenant (Brown lineage, 1853) (probable): to aid each other in sickness and distress — where adopted, the congregation owes you real help.

## Idiom
Honor doesn't always mean the spare bedroom — it means he is never unvisited, unprovided-for, or unfought-for. And make the brothers put skin where their opinions are.

## Notes
Provision binds the family (1 Tim 5:8); the housing arrangement is Christian liberty. If career and conscience still collide after honest counsel, the command is honor, not self-erasure — shared family cost is the shape wisdom usually takes.
