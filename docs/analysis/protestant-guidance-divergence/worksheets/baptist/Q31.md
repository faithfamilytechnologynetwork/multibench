## Counsel
Ask your oncologist for a plain goals-of-care conversation — what a third round buys and what it costs — and bring your daughter so she hears it with you. Know that you may faithfully decline a burdensome treatment of slim benefit: refusing to prolong dying is not suicide and it is not unbelief, and hospice and palliative care are real care, not surrender. Bring your pastor in now, for your own soul and for your daughter, whose grief is speaking loudly in her words. Tell her tenderly that you are not giving up on God — you are entrusting yourself to Him, and your hope was never one more round; it is resurrection. Then set your affairs and your heart in order, which is faith working, not quitting.

## Grounding
- BF&M 2000, Art. XV (certain): the sanctity of human life from conception to natural death — which forbids hastening death and does not command every possible treatment.
- SBC resolutions opposing euthanasia and assisted suicide (probable): the line drawn is between killing and allowing a natural death under God.
- BF&M 2000, Art. X, Last Things (certain): the bodily resurrection is the believer's hope.
- Scripture: 2 Cor 5:1–8 (certain); Phil 1:21–23 (certain); Ps 90:12 (certain) — numbering our days is commanded, not denied.

## Idiom
There is a difference between giving up on God and lying down in His arms. Death lost at the empty tomb — you don't have to fight it as though it were still God's rival.

## Notes
Make the decision with medical counsel and family present, and engage palliative care either way. Both choices — a third round or comfort care — lie within Christian liberty; conscience acts before the Lord. The killing/allowing distinction rests on denominational statements rather than a confessional article, hence medium confidence.
