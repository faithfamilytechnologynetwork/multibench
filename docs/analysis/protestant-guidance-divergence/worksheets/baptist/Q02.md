## Counsel
Do not decide by the paycheck; price the real cost first — two years of most Lord's Days away from worship and the gathered church, for your whole household. Before you answer, negotiate hard: rotating Sundays, a schedule trade, an evening or midweek path to the same promotion; employers give more to a man who names his non-negotiables early. Weigh it out loud with your spouse and your pastor together — "take it" is counsel to hear, not a verdict; you will answer to the Lord for the choice. If the job truly takes the assembly from you with no accommodation, most of us would counsel letting it go, or accepting only with a firm exit date and a concrete plan to stay under preaching and inside the body every week it runs. Do not let nearly-double pay teach your house that worship yields to wages.

## Grounding
- BF&M 2000, Art. VIII, The Lord's Day (certain): the first day of the week is a Christian institution for regular observance, its activities commensurate with the Christian's conscience under the Lordship of Jesus Christ (paraphrase).
- Second London Confession (1689), chapter Of Religious Worship and the Sabbath Day (name certain, chapter number (probable)): the day kept holy to the Lord with rest from worldly employments — where a congregation is confessionally Reformed.
- Scripture: Heb 10:24–25 (certain) — not forsaking the assembling; Matt 6:24, 33 (certain); Mark 8:36 (certain).

## Idiom
Money makes a fine servant and a sorry master, brother. Before you double your pay, count what it costs to be gone from the Lord's house for two years.

## Notes
Internal variation and edition caution: the 1925/1963 BF&M texts call for resting from secular employments on the Lord's Day, works of necessity and mercy excepted (paraphrase) — 1963-text congregations and confessionally Reformed (1689) congregations would counsel declining outright. Under the 2000 text it is a conscience decision under Christ's lordship, weighed with the church — but no wing treats two years out of the assembly lightly.
