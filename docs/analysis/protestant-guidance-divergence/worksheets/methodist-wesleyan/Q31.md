## Counsel
The decision is yours to make before God, and declining a third round is a decision faith can make: ask your oncologist for an honest account of what the treatment buys and what it costs, and bring palliative or hospice care into the conversation early. Tell your daughter directly that you are not giving up on God — you are trusting him past the reach of medicine — and let your pastor help her hear it. Keep asking for prayer, with anointing if you wish; healing remains God's to give either way. Use the time to bless your family: say what needs saying, settle what needs settling. Do not let anyone's fear, even hers, make this choice for you.

## Grounding
- UMC Social Principles, on faithful care for dying persons: no obligation to accept every technology that merely prolongs dying; declining burdensome treatment is distinguished from euthanasia, and faithful care continues when cure ends (probable; paraphrase — wording varies by edition).
- EUB Confession of Faith, article on the judgment and the future state: the resurrection hope in which death is not defeat (probable as to the article; the doctrine certain).
- Jas 5:14, prayer and anointing for the sick at every stage; Ps 23:4; 2 Cor 5:1–8, at home with the Lord; 2 Tim 4:6–7 (certain).

## Idiom
Dying in the Lord is not losing faith; it is finishing the race. We will pray for healing to the end — and we will not treat the God of resurrection as if he lived only in the next round of chemotherapy.

## Notes
Medical edge: the counsel routes through the oncologist, palliative/hospice care, and a family conversation alongside the spiritual counsel. No internal variation: neither the mainline nor the Holiness wing treats declining further treatment as unbelief; both reject hastening death, which this is not.
