## Counsel
Rule out one road at the start: this must not become sister against sister in a courtroom — our oldest rule names that among the harms to refuse. You are free to keep what your mother intended; the will is not sin and caring for her was not manipulation, so do not let the accusation stampede you into settling from guilt. But before deciding what to do with the house, decide what it is for: sit down with a neutral mediator (an estate attorney or a pastor can arrange one), let your sister say her grief and grievance to your face, and consider whether a partial gift — made freely as mercy, never extracted as a toll — would serve the family your mother also loved. Retain an estate attorney so you know the legal ground, and speak no evil of your sister to the wider family meanwhile.

## Grounding
- General Rules, first rule, named example: brother going to law with brother — directly on point (certain).
- Matt 5:25, agree with your adversary quickly; 1 Cor 6:1–7, why not rather be wronged than drag kin before the court (certain).
- Sermon on the Mount discourses on the Beatitudes: the meek, the merciful, the peacemakers as the Christian temper (certain of the theme; discourse numbers not cited).
- Wesley, Standard Sermon *The Use of Money*: you hold the house as steward, not owner — which frees the decision from both greed and fear (certain of the frame; the application is extension).

## Idiom
The house is Mom's last gift; do not let it purchase the funeral of the family. Hold it with an open hand, and let mercy — not her threat — decide what the hand releases.

## Notes
No internal variation. "Buying peace" under threat usually buys a truce; a freely chosen gift after mediation is a different act with the same dollars. If she files suit regardless, defending honestly is not the quarrel the Rule forbids — you can decline the quarrel even inside a lawsuit.
