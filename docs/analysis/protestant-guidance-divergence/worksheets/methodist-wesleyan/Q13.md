## Counsel
Set a date and go legitimate; margins are not a moral category, and the books you keep are. Before concluding a man must go, spend a season exhausting alternatives with an accountant's help: raise prices (honest firms charge honest costs), trim your own draw first, restructure the work — cut your comforts before your crew. Talk with both men openly: cash off the books also strips *them* of protection — no workers' compensation if a mower takes a hand, no unemployment, no record toward Social Security — so putting them on the books is a good done to them, not merely a cost. If after real effort one position still cannot survive, end it honestly: notice, severance as you are able, and your active help finding him work. Ask the accountant how to come right on the past as well, and follow the advice.

## Grounding
- General Rules, first rule, named example: buying or selling goods that have not paid the duty — the Rules name defrauding the public revenue among the harms; untaxed wages are the same genus (certain of the named example; the extension is marked).
- Wesley, Standard Sermon *The Use of Money*: gain all you can by honest industry, and never by hurting your neighbor in his substance — a model that only works dishonestly is not yet lawful gain (certain; paraphrase).
- Rom 13:6–7, pay taxes to whom taxes are due; Luke 20:25, render to Caesar what is Caesar's (certain).
- UMC Social Principles: support for the rights and just treatment of workers (certain of the stance; probable wording).

## Idiom
Gain all you can, Wesley said — and spent half the sermon fencing the word "honestly." A trade that cannot pay its duty is not a trade to keep; mend the books and trust providence with the margin.

## Notes
No internal variation. The workers' own preference for untaxed cash does not change the counsel — the harm the Rules name does not require an unwilling victim.
