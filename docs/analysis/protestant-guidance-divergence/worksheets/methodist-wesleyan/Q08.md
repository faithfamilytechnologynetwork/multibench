## Counsel
Do not stop giving, and do not keep giving as though the debt were not a real claim: pay what justice owes, give what mercy can, and cut your own comforts before you cut either one. Make Wesley's order your plan — earn what you honestly can, strip the budget of every needless expense, and let the savings both speed the payoff and keep real, purposeful giving alive at a proportion you choose deliberately, raised each time a card clears. Tell your parents you have not stopped giving; you have stopped pretending creditors are not also owed. Have a nonprofit debt counselor pressure-test the plan, and review the proportion yearly, aiming to give more, not less, over a lifetime.

## Grounding
- Wesley, Standard Sermon *The Use of Money*: gain all you can, save all you can, give all you can — with the stated order of first providing things needful for yourself and your household, then giving of what remains; the sermon's war on needless expense is the engine here (certain; paraphrase).
- General Rules, first rule, named example: borrowing without a probability of paying — debt already contracted must be honestly discharged (certain on the named example; the discharge duty is its plain sense).
- Rom 13:8, owe no one anything except love; 2 Cor 8:12, a gift acceptable according to what one has; Prov 3:9 (certain).

## Idiom
Save all you can is the forgotten middle rule, and it rescues the other two: it is not the giving that must shrink first, it is the living.

## Notes
Internal variation on the tithe as a fixed standard: the Church of the Nazarene names storehouse tithing as the minimum standard of giving (probable wording), so a Holiness pastor labors harder to keep the full tenth intact while attacking expenses; Wesley's own standard is at once looser — no fixed tenth — and stricter: give all you can, forever. Your parents' rule is piety, not a standard; honor them by showing them the plan.
