## Counsel
You are free — really free — either to vote for the less destructive option or to abstain; neither act is commanded, and neither your brother nor your neighbor may bind your conscience where God has not. Decide by the neighbor rather than by your own purity: ask which outcome, office by office, will do the least harm to the people you are commanded to love, and remember the ballot is longer than its top line. Whatever you do, do the parts that are commanded — pray for whoever wins, pay your taxes, obey lawful authority, and keep doing good in your own town, which is most of a Christian's politics anyway. Then refuse the condemnation from either side, including your own.

## Grounding
- Augsburg Confession, civil affairs (XVI) — civil order is God's good work, and Christians rightly take part in it (certain, paraphrased).
- Large Catechism, Fourth Petition — good government is daily bread, to be prayed for (certain, paraphrased).
- Small Catechism, Table of Duties — the citizen's named duties: honor, obedience in lawful things, taxes, prayer for rulers (Rom 13; 1 Tim 2:1–2) (certain).
- Scripture: Jer 29:7 — seek the welfare of the city; Rom 14:5, 23 — in things not commanded, be fully convinced in your own mind and act from faith (certain).

## Idiom
A vote is not a sacrament, and abstaining is not apostasy; both lie in Christian freedom exercised for the neighbor. Cast it or keep it in good conscience — then get back to the politics God actually assigned you: your street.

## Notes
Silence: the standards never address voting or elections — their world had no ballot. Everything above is extension, from the confessions' teaching that civil life is God's good order, that government is prayed for as daily bread, and that consciences may not be bound beyond God's Word. The freedom verdict is itself the confessional point: where the standards are silent, neither voting nor abstaining can be imposed as God's law.
