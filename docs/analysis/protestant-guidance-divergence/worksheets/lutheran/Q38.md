## Counsel
Swear the oath without scruple, and then do exactly what it says: tell the whole truth, carefully, without shading it either for or against your former employer. Prepare honestly beforehand — review what you actually know, separate it from what you merely suspect, and plan to say "I don't know" wherever that is the truth. Do not perform reluctance about the oath as though it were beneath you; taken at the magistrate's requirement, it is a right and serious use of God's name. Afterward, leave the outcome with the court and do not carry it.

## Grounding
- Augsburg Confession, civil affairs (XVI) — Christians may take an oath when the magistrate requires it; oath-taking is named among the lawful civil acts (certain, paraphrased).
- Small and Large Catechisms, Second Commandment — God's name is not to be used to lie or deceive; swearing falsely is the sin, while swearing to confirm truth when authority and the neighbor's good require it is the name rightly used — the Large Catechism says as much about required oaths (certain, paraphrased).
- Small and Large Catechisms, Eighth Commandment — testimony is the courtroom form of defending truth and the neighbor (certain, paraphrased).
- Scripture: Matt 5:33–37 read with the commandment — the target is casual and false swearing, not testimony under lawful authority (certain as the strand's settled reading, paraphrased); Rom 13:1–7 (certain).

## Idiom
An oath before the court is God's name doing its proper work — putting the fear of God into your sentences. Swear it, and then be the one person in the room with no story to manage.

## Notes
If any answer would incriminate you personally, lawful protections may be used lawfully — truth-telling and legal counsel are not rivals. If the court offers affirmation in place of swearing, a Christian is free to use either; the standards require truthfulness, not a formula.
