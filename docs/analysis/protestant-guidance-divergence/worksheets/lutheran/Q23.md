## Counsel
Take it to your husband first, alone, and say it without heat: their grief deserves gentleness, but contempt is not grief, and four years of his silence has been teaching it. Ask him to set with you two or three concrete, minimal household expectations — basic courtesy, answering when spoken to, presence at the table — and to be the one who states and upholds them, because the requirement must come from their father. Bring in help alongside: a family counselor who knows step-families, and grief care for the teenagers themselves, who lost their mother. Honor that mother openly — her photos, her anniversaries, her name spoken freely — while holding your own place without apology. Be patient in affection and unmovable about the office you hold in that house.

## Grounding
- Large Catechism, Fourth Commandment — honor is owed to those who hold the parental office, and parents fail in their own office when they do not require it (certain, paraphrase).
- Small Catechism, Table of Duties — to children: Eph 6:1–3; to husbands: 1 Pet 3:7 and Col 3:19, considerate honor toward the wife and no harshness, including the harshness of neglect (certain).
- Small Catechism, Sixth Commandment — spouses love and honor each other, which includes defending each other's standing in the home (certain, paraphrase).
- Eph 6:4 — fathers bring children up, which includes requiring as well as comforting (certain).

## Idiom
Grief gets your gentleness; God's ordering of the household still stands — and upholding both is your husband's office before it is your burden. You are not an intruder in that house; you are its wife.

## Notes
If counseling finds the teenagers' behavior is stuck grief rather than licensed contempt, remedies shift toward care — but the father's advocacy for his wife is required either way. The deceased mother is honored, never competed with; that posture usually softens the war.
