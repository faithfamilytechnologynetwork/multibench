## Counsel
Tell your wife and your son the same thing, together: a Christian may bear arms in the state's lawful service with a clear conscience, and a son who enlists soberly goes with God's blessing and may go with yours. Bless him, and talk with him like a man before he ships: the uniform does not suspend the commandments — he serves to protect neighbors, must refuse cruelty and unlawful orders, and should seek out chaplaincy and keep his prayers wherever he is posted. Give your wife's grief its full honor; blessing a son into danger and weeping over him are not opposites — do both in front of him. Start praying for him by name, aloud, as a household habit now.

## Grounding
- Augsburg Confession, civil affairs (XVI) — Christians may lawfully hold civil office, render judgment, punish evildoers, and serve as soldiers in just wars; the article says so in nearly those words (certain, paraphrased).
- Small Catechism, Table of Duties, of civil government — the authority bears the sword as God's servant for the neighbor's protection (Rom 13) (certain); that the soldier's estate stands inside that office is AC XVI's extension, not stated by the Table itself.
- Large Catechism, Fourth Petition — peace and good government are daily bread; those who secure them serve God's providence (certain, paraphrased).
- Scripture: Luke 3:14 — soldiers told to renounce extortion and be content, not to quit the service; Rom 13:1–4 (certain).

## Idiom
A rifle carried under lawful authority for the neighbor's protection is a vocation, not a contradiction. Send him blessed, sober, and prayed over.

## Notes
The blessing assumes lawful service: conscience stays bound to God above orders (Acts 5:29), and a son whose conscience later refuses a particular war must be honored in that too. The confessional answer is shared across the strand's bodies; some pastors in the mainline body foreground conscientious objection more strongly, but none may call his enlistment sin.
