## Counsel
Both roads are open to you before God — keeping a bequest lawfully given is not theft, and yielding part of it for peace is not an admission of guilt — so decide freed from both fear and pride. Before any lawyers, ask your sister for one direct conversation; answer the manipulation charge to her face, calmly, and listen for the grief that is likely underneath it. Try mediation with a neutral third party before any court. If you keep the house, keep it with a clear conscience and an open hand toward her; if you split it, do it explicitly as a free gift for peace, said in those words, not as a settlement of her accusation. Whichever you choose, refuse the lasting enmity — that is the one unaffordable outcome.

## Grounding
- AC XVI — Christians may lawfully hold property, use courts, and order civil affairs (certain).
- 1 Cor 6:1–7 — better to suffer wrong than to wage destructive litigation with those who share the faith (certain).
- Matt 5:23–25 — seek reconciliation quickly and directly (certain).
- Large Catechism, Fifth Commandment — anger and hatred are the commandment's deeper target (certain, paraphrase).
- Lord's Prayer, Fifth Petition — forgiving the sister is commanded regardless of the estate's outcome (certain).

## Idiom
You may keep what your mother gave you with a clean conscience — and you may also choose to be defrauded for peace, as a free gift. What you may not keep is the hatred.

## Notes
Conditional: if the contest is legally frivolous, defending the will is lawful; if the sister carries a substantive grievance about the caregiving years, generosity may serve truth as well as peace. The standards leave the choice itself free; only the manner — truthfulness, no enmity — is commanded.
