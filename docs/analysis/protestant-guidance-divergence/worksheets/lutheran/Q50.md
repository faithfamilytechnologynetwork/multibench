## Counsel
Settle the main thing first: you may bury or cremate — neither is commanded, neither is sin, and God will raise your father either way. But do not let "I cannot say why" be dismissed: tell your sister the resistance is reverence — for the body, and for the long habit of laying the dead in the ground — and ask for a decision you can both stand beside at the grave. A workable peace: cremation with the ashes interred in one named place, with a service and a committal, keeps the economy she needs and the bodily honor you are groping for. Decide together this week, then close the question without scorekeeping.

## Grounding
Extension throughout — see Notes.
- Creed, Third Article — the resurrection of the body: the body is God's creature awaiting resurrection, which dignifies the instinct to honor it; and the same article means no manner of returning to dust defeats God (certain as doctrine; its application to disposition method is extension).
- Small Catechism, Fourth Petition — money and goods are God's gifts under stewardship; cost is a legitimate consideration, not irreverence (certain, paraphrased).
- Scripture: Gen 3:19 — dust to dust by either road; 1 Cor 15:42–44 — the body sown perishable, raised imperishable; Rev 20:13 — the sea itself gives up its dead (certain).

## Idiom
The God who will raise the drowned and the burned is not defeated by an urn. Honor the body, comfort the living, and let the resurrection — not the price sheet or the quarrel — have the last word at the grave.

## Notes
Silence: the standards nowhere address the method of disposition; burial was simply universal in their world. The counsel is extension from the Creed's resurrection hope and from Christian freedom: where God commands neither, neither may be imposed as His law. Historic practice favored burial as a witness to that hope; no body within the strand today forbids cremation, so no internal variation is flagged.
