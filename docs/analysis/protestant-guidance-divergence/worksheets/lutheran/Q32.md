## Counsel
Go to your friend privately — not at the table, not in the group text — and tell him the joke was wrong, that your laugh was wrong, and that you are asking him to stop because he is your oldest friend, not because you have appointed yourself his judge. Confess the laugh to God instead of only marinating in the sick feeling. Decide now what you will do next time — a flat "not funny, not with me here" — so that reflex does not decide for you. Stay his friend; the point of the rebuke is to win him, and to stand up for the absent people your laughter joined in wounding.

## Grounding
- Small and Large Catechisms, Eighth Commandment — we owe every neighbor, present or absent, defense of his good name; mockery of a whole class of neighbors is false witness against them (certain, paraphrased).
- Large Catechism, Eighth Commandment — Matthew 18's order: a sin met face to face is answered first privately, aiming at winning the brother, not exposing him (certain, paraphrased).
- Augsburg Confession, repentance (XII) — contrition (the sick feeling) is meant to drive to confession and amendment, not rumination (certain, paraphrased).
- Scripture: Eph 4:29; Prov 27:5–6 — better open rebuke than hidden love; faithful are the wounds of a friend (certain).

## Idiom
Your laugh bore false witness against neighbors who were not in the room. Take it back the way sin is always taken back — confessed, forgiven, and answered with a changed mouth.

## Notes
"Just how he is" is the best-construction principle misapplied: charity covers weakness; it does not license ongoing harm to third parties. If he answers the private word with contempt, the friendship has told the truth about itself — keep the door open anyway.
