## Counsel
The conversation you need is with your husband, not the teenagers: tell him that four years is no longer grief's pace but avoidance's, and that by requiring nothing of his children he has left his wife alone in her own house. Ask him to come with you to a counselor who knows both grief and stepfamilies; this is past what resolve alone will fix. Agree together on a floor — basic courtesy from the children, named consequences, delivered by him and not by you — while you keep honouring their mother out loud: her pictures stay, her name is welcome, and you say plainly that you are not and will not pretend to be her replacement. Be patient with their grief and immovable about your place. And pray for the two of them by name; they did lose their mother, and that truth can stand beside yours.

## Grounding
- Marriage rite: mutual society, help, and comfort, in prosperity and adversity — his vow obliges him to stand with his wife in this (certain substance).
- Homily "Of the state of Matrimony" (Second Book, listed in Article XXXV): the husband charged with cherishing care of his wife and the keeping of household concord (certain title; probable content).
- Fifth commandment; Catechism: children owe honour and ordered submission within the household to those set over them (certain substance; application to a stepmother probable).
- Scripture: Col 3:19, 21 — husbands, love your wives; fathers, provoke not your children — both duties his (certain).

## Idiom
He married you into that house; the Prayer Book expects him to make it your home. Grief is honoured by truth, not by handing it the keys to the parlour.

## Notes
The standards give the marriage duty and the household order; stepfamily technique — the counseling, the courtesy floor — is prudential extension. Press for help for the teenagers' own grief as care, not correction; it honours their mother too.
