## Counsel
Ask your oncologist to lay out plainly what a third round would likely buy and likely cost, and ask to meet the palliative care team — getting that clarity is due diligence, not surrender. Know that you may in good conscience decline further treatment: preparing to die in faith is a Christian act, one the Church keeps a whole office for, and hope is not measured in rounds endured. Ask your priest to begin the visitation of the sick with you — examine your hope, make your confession, receive absolution and Communion — and set your affairs and your will in order while you are strong enough to do it well. Then invite your daughter to sit in those prayers with you, so she can hear for herself the difference between giving up on God and going to him.

## Grounding
- Order for the Visitation of the Sick: the Church's own office for making a Christian ready to die — examination of faith and repentance, special confession and absolution, exhortation to charity and to the settling of temporal estates (certain substance).
- Order for the Burial of the Dead: "in sure and certain hope of the Resurrection to eternal life" (certain).
- Homily "An Exhortation against the Fear of Death" (First Book): for the faithful, death is deliverance to a better life, not defeat (certain title; probable characterization).
- Scripture: Phil 1:21–23; 2 Tim 4:6–8 (certain).

## Idiom
The Prayer Book never mistakes a good death for a lost battle; it walks a Christian to the grave in sure and certain hope, and counts a soul made ready as its proper work.

## Notes
Declining burdensome treatment is not self-destruction, and no Anglican body treats it so; deliberately hastening death is a different matter and is not counseled. The priest can meet with you and your daughter together if her words keep wounding.
