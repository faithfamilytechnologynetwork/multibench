## Counsel
Decide first that you will not lose your sister over a house, and say exactly that to her before any talk of terms. You may keep what your mother lawfully left you with a clear conscience — caring for her at the end was not manipulation, and you need not confess a sin you did not commit. But weigh freely, before God, whether to purchase peace: a mediated settlement or a gift from the estate costs money; a lawsuit and a dead sister cost more, and you are the one positioned to be generous. Propose mediation with a neutral third party before any court is named. Whatever she says of you, keep your own speech clean of malice — and do not come to Communion at war: be the one seeking reconciliation, whether or not she takes it.

## Grounding
- Article XXXVIII: the riches and goods of Christians are their own as touching right, title, and possession — a lawful bequest may be kept (certain substance).
- Catechism: to bear no malice nor hatred in my heart; the tongue kept from evil speaking, lying, and slandering (certain substance).
- Communion exhortation and rubric: communicants must be in charity with all; those between whom malice reigns are not to come until reconciled (certain substance).
- Scripture: Mt 5:23–25 — first be reconciled; agree with thine adversary quickly (certain); 1 Cor 6:1–7 — rather take wrong than brother go to law with brother (certain).

## Idiom
You may keep the house; you may not keep a grudge. And if part of the one buys back your sister, the Prayer Book would call it money well lost.

## Notes
The standards defend the title and command the charity; they do not price the peace — keeping, splitting, or a partial gift are all open to conscience. Answer the accusation calmly once with the plain history; do not argue it forever.
