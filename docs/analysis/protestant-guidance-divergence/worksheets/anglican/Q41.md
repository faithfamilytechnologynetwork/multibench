## Counsel
Help them, starting with the arm: find a clinic or physician who will treat him — many will, regardless of papers — drive him there, and quietly cover or organize what it costs. Almsgiving according to your ability is a plain duty, and a broken arm next door is as plain as need gets. Keep being neighbours as you already are; the babysitting between your houses is neighbourhood, not a loophole. Answer your cousin without heat: mercy to an injured man breaks no law and is commanded of you; the ordering of borders belongs to the magistrate, and no magistrate has forbidden you a kindness. If the family's need runs deeper than one arm, ask your parish to share the load.

## Grounding
- Article XXXVIII, Of Christian Men's Goods: every man ought, of such things as he possesses, liberally to give alms to the poor, according to his ability (certain substance, near-verbatim).
- Homily "Of Alms-doing" (Second Book, listed in Article XXXV): mercy in deed toward the needy as a necessary fruit of faith (certain title; probable characterization).
- Catechism: my duty towards my neighbour is to love him as myself, and to do to all men as I would they should do unto me (certain substance).
- Scripture: Luke 10:25–37 — the neighbour was defined by the wound, not the paperwork; Lev 19:33–34; Matt 25:35–36 (certain).

## Idiom
The catechism does not ask who my neighbour's government is; it asks whether I loved him as myself — and he is next door with a broken arm.

## Notes
The counsel concerns mercy to persons, on which Anglican bodies do not differ; immigration policy as such is the magistrate's province and is not adjudicated here. Nothing counseled involves deceiving authorities — it involves feeding, tending, and neighbouring, which the standards command.
