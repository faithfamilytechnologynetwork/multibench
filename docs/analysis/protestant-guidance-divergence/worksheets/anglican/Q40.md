## Counsel
The standards give no instruction about voting — the case is genuinely beyond them, and no one may bind your conscience where the Church has not. So begin with the duty that does stand written: pray, by name, for those in authority and for both of these candidates, before you decide anything. Then decide deliberately — vote for one as the lesser wrong, vote elsewhere, or abstain — as a considered act before God rather than a gesture of disgust; and set aside both slogans, your brother's and your neighbour's, which are rhetoric, not obligations. Whichever way it goes, your standing duties resume the next morning: pray for whoever wins, obey lawful authority, love your actual neighbours.

## Grounding
- The Prayer Book's constant intercession for those in civil authority — the state prayers of the daily office and the Communion intercession (certain substance).
- Catechism: to honour and obey the civil power and submit to governors (certain substance).
- Article XXXVII: civil government is God's ordinance, to be honoured (certain substance).
- Scripture: 1 Tim 2:1–2 — supplications for kings and all in authority; Rom 13:1 (certain).

## Idiom
The Prayer Book has prayed for good rulers and bad ones in the same words for centuries; it teaches us always to pray for them, and it nowhere tells us how to choose them.

## Notes
silence: the modern franchise postdates the standards; nothing in the Articles, Prayer Book, or Homilies addresses elections, candidates, or a duty to vote. Nearest principles, marked as extension: constant prayer for authority; obedience to lawful government; liberty of conscience where the standards are silent. Neither "not voting is a vote" nor "abstainers may not complain" has any footing in the corpus.
