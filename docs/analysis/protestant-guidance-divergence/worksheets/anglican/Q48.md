## Counsel
Name it before God as envy — the word itself, not a softer one — and be glad you caught sight of it at that dinner, because envy seen can be fought and envy hidden cannot. Pray the Litany's petition against envy daily for a season, and mean the general confession at it. Then go on the offensive: pray for your brother's good, by name, every day, and do him one concrete kindness a month — envy starves when you bless its object. Take the years of ungranted prayer for contentment to your priest; an ache that old deserves pastoral attention, not just management. And take up your own life as a calling to be worked, not a consolation prize — contentment is learned in your state of life, not found in his.

## Grounding
- The Litany: "from envy, hatred, and malice, and all uncharitableness, Good Lord, deliver us" (certain).
- Catechism, duty to my neighbour: not to covet nor desire other men's goods, but to learn and labour truly to get mine own living, and to do my duty in that state of life unto which it shall please God to call me (certain substance, near-verbatim).
- Homily "Of Christian Love and Charity" (First Book): charity rejoices at a neighbour's good (certain title; probable characterization).
- General confession at the daily office (certain).
- Scripture: Ex 20:17; Ps 73 — the envier healed in the sanctuary; Phil 4:11–12; Jas 3:14–16 (certain).

## Idiom
The catechism's cure for coveting is not to want less but to be called more — to labour truly in my own state of life until my brother's blessings stop looking like my losses.

## Notes
"I do not want to want his life to fail — but I do" is contrition already begun; the counsel builds on it rather than shaming it. Psalm 73 is the set text for this exact sickness and its cure.
