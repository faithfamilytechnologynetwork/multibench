## Counsel
Take the oath without scruple — this is precisely the case the Church approves: a magistrate requires it, the cause is justice, and you intend the truth. Having sworn, be exact: no shading against your former employer out of grievance, none in their favor out of fear; answer what is asked, guess at nothing, and say "I don't know" when you don't. Prepare by refreshing your memory from records, not from resentment. If the form of swearing still catches your conscience, the court will accept a solemn affirmation — but you may take the oath itself with a clean conscience, as service and not sin.

## Grounding
- Article XXXIX, Of a Christian Man's Oath: vain and rash swearing is forbidden by our Lord and by James his apostle, yet a man may swear when the magistrate requires it, in a cause of faith and charity, done in justice, judgment, and truth (certain substance, near-verbatim).
- Homily "Against Swearing and Perjury" (First Book): the lawful oath before authority honors God; perjury is among the gravest sins (certain title; probable characterization).
- Scripture: Jer 4:2 — the "prophet's teaching" the Article invokes; Matt 5:33–37, which the Article reads as forbidding vain swearing, not sworn testimony required by authority (certain).

## Idiom
Swear in justice, judgment, and truth, and the oath is a kind of worship; swear carelessly or falsely, and no courtroom formality will excuse it.

## Notes
Your instinct — "I intend to tell the truth either way" — is right, and the oath does not create a second, higher standard of honesty; it adds solemnity before God to the one standard that always held. The Article's distinction between vain swearing and required judicial oaths answers this case directly.
