## Counsel
Succour your father — that duty is fixed; its form is not, and you have more faithful options than the two starkest. His savings exist for exactly this: a good home paid from his own means, chosen with care and visited often and faithfully, can be true honour — and so can care in your house with real help brought in; do not assume the whole load falls on you because you are the willing one. Call the family meeting now and turn your brothers' opinions into assignments: who gives money, who gives regular time, who holds power of attorney. Get professional counsel early — a medical assessment of what the dementia will need, an elder-law adviser — and settle his affairs (will, powers, wishes) while he can still speak to them. Choose so that you can sit by his bed at the end unashamed; that is the test, not the address.

## Grounding
- Catechism, duty towards my neighbour: to love, honour, and succour my father and mother (certain substance).
- Fifth commandment as rehearsed at Holy Communion, with its response (certain); Mk 7:9–13 — the Lord condemns devices that excuse withholding help owed to parents (certain).
- Scripture: 1 Tim 5:4 — let children learn to requite their parents; 1 Tim 5:8 — providing for one's own (certain).
- Visitation of the Sick: the church attends decline with prayer, and would have the sick person's worldly affairs put in order in good time (certain substance).

## Idiom
The Catechism's word is succour — real relief, really given. Whether the bed stands in your house or in a good home matters less than whether your face is often above it.

## Notes
Spending his savings on his care is right stewardship of his goods, not the loss of an inheritance. If you take him in, guard your own health and take respite help — a burned-out daughter succours no one. Your career may lawfully weigh in choosing the form; it may not silence the duty.
