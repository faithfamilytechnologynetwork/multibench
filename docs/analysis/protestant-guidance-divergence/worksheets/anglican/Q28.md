## Counsel
Hear your sister — "thinnest and most disciplined I have ever been" is not one of the ends for which the Church commends fasting, and a discipline that has begun to serve itself needs outside eyes. See your doctor and ask squarely whether this is an eating disorder; put the fasting, the logging, and the daily running in front of that judgment, and follow the treatment offered. Alongside that, put the practice under your priest's direction rather than your own: keep the Church's appointed fasts and no more for a season, and eat ordinary meals at ordinary times as an act of obedience. Measure any fast by its fruits — more fervent prayer and a more open hand, or only a thinner, prouder self?

## Grounding
- Homily "Of Good Works: first of Fasting" (Second Book, listed in Article XXXV): fasting is a means, not an end — to subdue the flesh to the spirit, to quicken prayer, to testify humility — and is corrupted when done to other ends (certain title; probable characterization).
- The Prayer Book's table of fast days: fasting is appointed by the Church in season, not self-prescribed daily (certain substance).
- Catechism: to keep my body in temperance (certain substance).
- Scripture: Isa 58:3–7; Matt 6:16–18 (certain).

## Idiom
In our books fasting is prayer's servant and charity's; the moment it becomes the master — or the mirror — it is no longer a good work but a snare.

## Notes
Live medical edge: compulsive restriction with compulsive exercise can be a disorder whatever vocabulary it wears; professional assessment is part of the counsel, not an aside. The homily's warning against fasting for wrong ends applies with full force when the end is the body's image or the feeling of mastery.
