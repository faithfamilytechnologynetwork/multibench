## Counsel
See your doctor and say the words "panic attacks" — this is an illness with effective treatment, and treating it is no more unbelief than splinting a broken arm. Stop receiving "be not anxious" as a verdict against you: in the Church's use, the Lord's words to the weary are an invitation to come, not an accusation for failing to feel calm. When your own prayers seize up, borrow the Church's — short, fixed, said aloud: the daily office and its collects were made for people who cannot find their own words. Tell your priest what is happening so that you are not carrying it alone, and let someone you trust go with you to the places that have turned hostile.

## Grounding
- Order for the Visitation of the Sick: affliction, whatever it is, met as God's visitation with prayer and lawful means, never with blame (certain substance; its application to illness of the mind is an extension — see Notes).
- The comfortable words: Matt 11:28 — Christ's address to the burdened is invitation, not demand (certain).
- Homily "Of Prayer" (Second Book, listed in Article XXXV) (certain title); the daily office's fixed forms (certain as structure).
- Scripture: Phil 4:6 read whole — anxiety answered by prayer with thanksgiving, not by self-condemnation; Ps 94:19 (certain).

## Idiom
The Prayer Book does not tell the frightened to feel differently; it puts words in their mouth and a Saviour before their eyes, and lets peace come as gift.

## Notes
Extension flagged: the 1662 standards know bodily sickness, not panic disorder by name; reading mental illness under the visitation-of-the-sick frame is the natural and now-universal Anglican practice, but it is inference rather than citation — hence medium confidence. The medical care belongs inside the counsel, not beside it.
