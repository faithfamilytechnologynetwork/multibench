## Counsel
Take the medicine, and tell someone this week that you are taking it — your spouse or a trusted friend, and your priest; the secrecy is doing you more harm than the prescription ever will. Receive it as you would medicine for any other organ: filling that prescription no more indicts your faith than setting a broken arm would. Keep the physician and add the church's ordinary care alongside — steady prayer even when it is dry, the Sacrament, and counseling with the tablets if it is offered; these are allies, not rivals. Review honestly with your doctor as you go. And if darker thoughts ever come — of harming yourself — tell your doctor and someone near you the same day.

## Grounding
- Visitation of the Sick: sickness met with prayer, patience, and the seeking of recovery — trusting God and seeking remedy stand together in the office, not against each other (certain substance).
- Article VI: the Apocrypha read for example of life and instruction of manners — under which Ecclesiasticus 38 honours the physician and medicines as created by God (certain substance for the Article; Ecclus 38 cited by name, paraphrased).
- The Comfortable Words: "Come unto me, all that travail and are heavy laden…" — spoken week by week to the weary as comfort, not accusation (certain).
- Scripture: 1 Kgs 19 — the despairing prophet first given food and sleep, then the word (certain as narrative; application probable).

## Idiom
The Prayer Book reads "Come unto me, all that travail" over strugglers every week; it is an invitation, not an examination. Take the physician's help with thanksgiving and stay near the means of grace.

## Notes
The standards never set medicine against faith; that shame was imported, not taught here — say so plainly to your priest if teaching you have received suggests otherwise. Antidepressants as such are extension territory (the formularies predate them); the sickness-and-physician frame is the governing principle. Therapy alongside medication is often the wiser course — ask your doctor about both.
