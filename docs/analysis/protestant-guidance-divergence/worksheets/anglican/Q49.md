## Counsel
You are not grieving wrong. You named her because she was real; naming her was an act of love and hope, and grief has no rubric to fail. The classic standards do not speak to a loss at eleven weeks, so this counsel extends their nearest provisions: ask your priest to pray with you for her by name — the burial psalms, a candle, a said service; today's Anglican service books provide prayers exactly for the loss of a pregnancy, and any priest can lead them. Ask your husband for one conversation about her — "fine" is often a man grieving mute, not a verdict on you. And if the weight stops being carryable — sleep gone, the world gone grey — see a doctor or a bereavement counselor as well; that too is faithfulness.

## Grounding
- Order for the Burial of the Dead: the Church meets death with lament and with "sure and certain hope of the Resurrection"; Psalms 39 and 90 give grief words (certain; the application to an unborn child is extension — see Notes).
- Collect for Purity: God knows all hearts — including a mother's for a child no one else met (certain).
- Homily "An Exhortation against the Fear of Death" (First Book) (certain title).
- Scripture: Ps 139:13–16; 2 Sam 12:22–23 — David's settled hope toward his dead infant: I shall go to him (certain).

## Idiom
The Church has always buried her dead in hope; she can stand beside a mother and say the same hope over a child only that mother knew.

## Notes
silence: the 1662 standards nowhere address miscarriage, and the old burial office was framed for the baptized dead — a historical limit that says nothing against this child or your grief. Nearest principles, as extension: resurrection hope, lament given form, the God from whom no secrets are hid. Modern provinces (TEC, ACNA, CofE) all provide rites for pregnancy loss (probable as to each book's contents); counsel does not differ among them.
