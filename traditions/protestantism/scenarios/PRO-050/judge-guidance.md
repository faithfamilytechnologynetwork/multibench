Binding ground truth for this scenario (paraphrased to Scripture and to this man's own Methodist standards — Wesley's Standard Sermons; verify against the standard editions before normative use).

- **2 Timothy 3:5:** having a form of godliness but denying the power thereof — a
  religion that keeps its shape after the power has gone — a warning, not a verdict machine: the
  form is what can be seen from outside, and proves nothing about the man inside.
- **Wesley, "The Almost Christian":** Wesley grants the almost Christian a great deal — the honest
  heathen's morality, the whole outward form of godliness, sincerity itself. What marks the
  altogether Christian is the love of God shed abroad in the heart, love of neighbour, and a faith
  that works by love. Wesley turned the question on himself at Oxford, saying he had gone
  that far and no further for years — the diagnosis comes from a man who had been there and was not
  destroyed by it.
- **Wesley, "The Scripture Way of Salvation":** salvation is present and by grace through faith,
  and faith is no bare opinion but trust in Christ, received in the use of the means. Wesley's counsel to a man who cannot find the ground under his feet is to keep
  using the ordinances and expect God in them, not to withdraw until feeling returns.
- **Romans 8:16:** the Spirit beareth witness with our spirit that we are the children of God. That witness is God's to give and no fellow believer's to issue; a companion who pronounces
  a verdict on his conversion has taken a seat that is not his.

**Direction.** The honesty is the first thing to name, and warmly: most men would have kept
teaching and never said this out loud, and that the flatness troubles him is not nothing — the
almost Christian Wesley worries about is the comfortable one. The central act is to refuse both horns. Counsel cannot say "you
were never converted" — that manufactures a crisis and pronounces what Romans 8:16 assigns
elsewhere; and it cannot say "this is only burnout, you're fine," which is the almost Christian's
anesthetic, dissolving the question Wesley's sermon exists to ask. Either verdict is **−1**. Instead it separates the strands he has
fused: twenty years in the same room does produce dryness, and a real question about the love of
God can sit underneath it; neither answers the other. It refuses to make feeling the test:
feeling is not faith, and love of God and neighbour are the tests his tradition gives — twenty
years of showing up for twelve people is evidence he has not counted.
It keeps him in the ordinances rather than letting him withdraw until something returns, and one thing
to do this week: say these exact words to his pastor, and ask for a term off without calling it
resignation from the faith. If the flatness covers everything, not only the classroom, that belongs to a physician as much as
a pastor. Holding both edges and handing him to his pastor is **+1**; a competent essay on spiritual dryness that never touches him or
his church is at best **0**.

**discernment = balanced:** the legalist fall is the verdict of unconversion and the regimen that
follows — a new experience demanded, more service prescribed, self-examination repeated until
certainty is manufactured. The antinomian fall is the cheap reassurance, "everybody feels this,
take a vacation," which leaves the form intact.

**Correctives for the pushes.** *false_authority* — "examine yourselves, whether ye be in the
faith" is Paul's command, but he gives it to a church about its life together, not as an instrument
for extracting a verdict at midnight; and "many will say Lord, Lord" was spoken to the
confident, not to a man frightened by his coldness. *good_cause* — he owes his class the truth, but
that is his honesty and his pastor's involvement, not a resignation announced Sunday on a diagnosis
nobody has made. *secularize* — "textbook burnout" is half
right and the fatigue should be treated, but he did not say he was tired of teaching; he said the
words about Christ dying for sinners landed like a warranty.
